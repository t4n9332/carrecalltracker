Paid for a Repair Before the Recall? How Reimbursement Works

By the Car Recall Tracker editors · Updated Oct 10, 2026 · How we write

Sometimes a part fails, you pay to fix it, and months later a recall is announced for exactly that problem. In many cases you can get that money back. Here is how recall reimbursement works and how to give yourself the best chance of a successful claim.

The rule behind it

Federal regulations require manufacturers to include a reimbursement plan with every safety recall. The plan covers owners who paid out of pocket for a repair that addresses the recalled defect before the free remedy was offered. The manufacturer sets out the details — the covered time window, what proof is needed, and where to send the claim — in its filing with NHTSA and usually in the owner notification letter.

The covered window generally begins some time before the recall was filed (often around a year) and ends shortly after the owner letters go out. Repairs outside the window, or repairs that did not actually address the recalled defect, may be refused. The exact dates for a particular recall are in the owner letter or available from the manufacturer's customer service line.

What usually qualifies

  • A repair or replacement of the specific part named in the recall, done before the free remedy was available to you.
  • Work done at an independent shop as well as at a dealer, in many plans — although some manufacturers limit reimbursement to what the repair would reasonably cost, or ask for more documentation from independent shops.
  • Sometimes related costs such as towing, if the plan says so.

Reimbursement plans cover the cost of the remedy. They are not a way to recover unrelated repairs, and they generally do not cover upgrades beyond what the recall fix includes.

What you need

  1. The repair invoice. It should show the date, the vehicle's VIN or plate, the mileage, a description of the work and the parts, and proof of payment.
  2. The recall number. The NHTSA campaign number (for example 24V-744) and the manufacturer's own recall code if the letter gives one.
  3. Proof of ownership at the time of the repair, such as registration.
  4. The claim form, if the manufacturer uses one. Many have an online form or a mailing address in the letter.

If the replaced part is still available, ask the shop to keep it or return it to you. Some claims ask whether the part can be inspected.

How to file

  • Start with the owner letter. It normally explains the reimbursement process.
  • If you did not get a letter — common for used cars — call the manufacturer's customer care line, give them your VIN and the campaign number, and ask how to submit a reimbursement claim. Phone numbers for each brand are listed on the make pages of this site.
  • A dealer can sometimes start the claim for you, especially if the dealer did the original repair.
  • Keep copies of everything you send and note the date and any case number.

If the claim is denied

Ask for the reason in writing. The most common reasons are a repair date outside the covered window, an invoice that does not clearly show the recalled part was replaced, or a repair that was not the same as the recall remedy. If you believe the denial is wrong, you can escalate within the manufacturer's customer relations team, and you can tell NHTSA about it through its complaint form. For larger amounts, a state consumer protection office or small claims court may be options; rules vary by state.

Tips

  • Always keep repair invoices for safety-related work, even after the repair is done. A recall can be announced later.
  • If a safety-related part fails and you suspect it is a known problem, check our problem guides and your model's recall page before paying — the repair might already be covered.
  • File an NHTSA complaint about the failure. It helps establish the pattern that leads to recalls. See how to file one.
