Over-the-Air Recall Fixes: How Software Recalls Work

By the Car Recall Tracker editors · Updated Oct 10, 2026 · How we write

More and more recalls are fixed with a software update delivered over a cellular or Wi-Fi connection, with no dealer visit at all. These are still full safety recalls, with the same legal obligations — but there are a few things owners should check to make sure the fix actually reached their car.

Is a software update really a recall?

Yes, when it fixes a safety defect. NHTSA's position is that if a manufacturer determines a vehicle has a safety-related defect, it must file a recall report, even if the remedy is a remote software update. That means the recall gets a public campaign number, appears in VIN lookups until the update is installed, and owners must be notified.

Software-related recalls cover a wide range of problems: rear camera images that fail to display, warning lights or chimes that do not work, instrument clusters that go blank, power-steering or brake calibration issues, and driver-assistance features that brake when they should not, or do not brake when they should.

How over-the-air (OTA) fixes are delivered

  • The manufacturer pushes the update to vehicles in the recall population.
  • The car downloads it in the background, usually over its own cellular connection or your home Wi-Fi.
  • Installation typically needs the car to be parked, sometimes for 20 minutes or longer, and you may need to accept a prompt on the screen or in the brand's app.
  • Once installed, the manufacturer's records — and eventually the VIN lookup — show the recall as completed.

Not every software recall can be done remotely. Many vehicles can only receive updates for some modules over the air, and others need a dealer to connect a diagnostic tool. The remedy section of the recall notice says which applies.

What can go wrong

  • The update never downloads. Weak signal where the car is parked, an expired connected-services plan, or a disabled connection setting can block it.
  • The update waits for you. Some cars hold the update until the owner approves it on the screen or in the app.
  • The install fails. Low battery voltage is a common reason. The car may retry later or ask you to visit a dealer.
  • The car has been offline. Vehicles in storage or with the telematics module disconnected will miss updates.

How to confirm the fix

  1. Read the recall notice to find the target software version, if it is given.
  2. Check the software version in the vehicle's settings menu or the brand's app.
  3. Run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls a few days after the update. If the recall still shows as open, call the manufacturer's customer care line or book a dealer appointment — dealers can install the update directly.

Buying a used car with software recalls

A used car that has been sitting on a lot, or one whose connected-services account was not transferred, may have missed several updates. Ask the dealer to confirm all software recalls are complete, and run the VIN yourself. See our guide to buying a used car with an open recall.

Are OTA recalls less serious?

Not necessarily. The delivery method says nothing about the risk. A software fault in braking, steering or air bag control can be as serious as a mechanical one. Read the consequence paragraph of the notice, and the plain-English explanation on the recall page here, to understand what the defect does.

What OTA fixes do change is completion: remote updates can reach a large share of affected vehicles within weeks, while dealer-only recalls often take much longer because each owner has to book an appointment. That is a real safety benefit — as long as the update actually installs.
