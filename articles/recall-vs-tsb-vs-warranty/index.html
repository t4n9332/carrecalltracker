Recall vs. TSB vs. Warranty Extension: What's the Difference?

By the Car Recall Tracker editors · Updated Oct 10, 2026 · How we write

Recall, technical service bulletin, warranty extension, customer satisfaction program — manufacturers use several names for "we know about this problem". Only one of them is a safety recall, and the difference decides who pays for the repair and whether it is required by law.

Safety recall

A safety recall is the formal process under federal law for defects that create a risk to motor vehicle safety, or for vehicles that fail a Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard. It is filed with NHTSA, gets a public campaign number, and the manufacturer must notify owners by mail and fix the problem for free.

  • Who pays: the manufacturer, regardless of mileage or whether your warranty has expired (for vehicles up to 15 years old).
  • Is it public: yes. Every safety recall is searchable on nhtsa.gov and on this site.
  • Does ownership matter: no. Second and third owners are covered just like the first.

Recalls are about safety, not comfort or reliability. A transmission that shifts roughly is not a recall; a transmission that can unexpectedly shift into neutral while driving is.

Technical service bulletin (TSB)

A TSB is a set of repair instructions the manufacturer sends to its dealers: "if a customer comes in with this symptom, here is the known cause and the approved fix." TSBs cover all kinds of problems — noises, warning lights that come on by mistake, software glitches, leaks — and most are not safety-related.

  • Who pays: you, unless the vehicle is still under warranty or the manufacturer decides to cover it as goodwill. A TSB does not by itself create any obligation to fix your car for free.
  • Is it public: manufacturers must send copies of these communications to NHTSA, and they are listed under "manufacturer communications" when you look up a vehicle on nhtsa.gov.
  • Why it helps you: if your car has a symptom that matches a TSB, mentioning it to the service writer can save diagnostic time. If you are just outside the warranty, a documented known issue can strengthen a request for goodwill coverage.

Sometimes a TSB comes first and a recall follows, once the manufacturer or NHTSA concludes that the problem is a safety risk after all.

Warranty extension or special coverage

When a part fails more often than expected but the failure is not a safety defect, manufacturers sometimes extend the warranty on that one part — for example, covering a specific component to 10 years or 150,000 miles. Owners are usually told by letter. These programs are voluntary and their terms (mileage and time limits) are set by the manufacturer.

Customer satisfaction program

A customer satisfaction or service campaign is a free fix the manufacturer offers for a non-safety issue, often a software update or a part that causes complaints. Like warranty extensions, these are voluntary and often have an end date. If you get a letter labelled "customer satisfaction program", it is worth doing while it is free, but it is not a safety recall.

What about state lemon laws?

Lemon laws are state laws that usually apply to new vehicles with a serious defect the dealer cannot fix after a reasonable number of attempts within a set period. A recall on its own does not make a car a lemon, and the rules differ a lot from state to state. If a recall repair fails repeatedly on a fairly new car, your state attorney general's consumer protection office is a good place to find the rules that apply.

How to use this

  • Check for open safety recalls with your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Then read what each one means on your model's page here.
  • For a problem that is not a recall, ask the dealer whether there is a TSB or special coverage for your symptom and VIN.
  • If a problem looks like a safety risk and there is no recall, file a complaint with NHTSA. Our watchlist shows models with many complaints in an area that has no recall yet.
