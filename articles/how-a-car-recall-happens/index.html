How a Car Recall Happens, From First Complaint to Free Repair

By the Car Recall Tracker editors · Updated Oct 10, 2026 · How we write

Most owners only see the end of a recall: a letter in the mail saying a part needs to be replaced. Behind that letter is a process set by federal law, with deadlines the manufacturer has to meet. Knowing how it works makes it easier to understand why some recalls take months to fix and what you can do in the meantime.

1. A problem shows up

Recalls start from several directions. A manufacturer's own engineers may spot a pattern in warranty claims, field reports from dealers, or crash and injury data it is required to report. Suppliers find defects in parts they make for several brands at once, which is why one faulty air bag inflator or seat belt part can lead to recalls across many makes. And owners report problems directly to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) through its online complaint form.

A single complaint rarely triggers anything. What matters is a pattern: many reports of the same failure, on the same part, under similar conditions. That is why filing a complaint after a real safety problem is worth doing even if your car has already been repaired — your report becomes part of the pattern. Our article on how to file a safety complaint walks through it.

2. Someone decides it is a safety defect

Under the federal vehicle safety law, a recall is required when a vehicle or piece of equipment has a defect related to motor vehicle safety, or does not comply with a Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard. "Safety-related" is the key phrase. A rattle, a bad paint job or a radio that freezes is a quality problem. A brake line that can leak, a fuel pump that can stop the engine at speed, or a seat belt that may not latch is a safety problem.

There are two ways the decision gets made:

  • The manufacturer decides on its own. This is how the large majority of recalls begin. Once a manufacturer determines a safety defect exists, it has five business days to file a defect report with NHTSA, known as a Part 573 report.
  • NHTSA pushes for it. NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation screens complaints and other data. It can open a preliminary evaluation, escalate to an engineering analysis, and ultimately ask the manufacturer to recall. Most manufacturers agree at that stage; if one refuses, NHTSA can seek a court order, which is rare.

3. The recall is filed and gets a number

The Part 573 report describes the defect, the vehicles involved, the risk, and the planned fix. NHTSA assigns a campaign number such as 24V-744: the first two digits are the year, the letter is the type (V for vehicles, E for equipment, T for tires, C for child seats), and the last digits are a sequence number. That number is the easiest way to look up a recall, and it is printed on every recall page on this site. Our guide to reading a recall notice explains the other fields.

The report also lists the population — the range of vehicles that might have the defect. A recall often covers only part of a model year, for example vehicles built at one plant between two dates. That is why checking your own VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls matters more than the model year alone.

4. Owners are notified

Manufacturers must mail notification letters to registered owners within 60 days of filing the defect report. The letter explains the problem, the risk, any warning signs, and what to do. If the fix is not ready yet, the manufacturer sends an interim letter first and a second letter once parts are available.

The letters go to the address on your state vehicle registration, so a car bought used, or an owner who has moved, can miss them. Checking your VIN a couple of times a year — or following your model's page here by RSS — closes that gap.

When a defect is serious enough that the vehicle should not be used, the letter says so plainly: either do not drive the vehicle until it is repaired, or park it outside and away from buildings because of a fire risk. These advisories are rare but they are the ones to act on the same day.

5. The free remedy

The manufacturer must offer a remedy at no cost to the owner. It can repair the vehicle, replace the defective part or vehicle, or in some cases refund the purchase price. The free-remedy requirement applies to vehicles up to 15 years old, counted from the first sale. Older vehicles can still be included in a recall and many manufacturers fix them anyway, but the law does not require it.

The repair is done by a franchised dealer for the brand. You do not need to go back to the dealer that sold you the car, and you do not need to be the original owner. If you already paid for a repair that the recall later covers, you may be able to get your money back — see how recall reimbursement works.

6. Waiting for parts

For large recalls, the fix is often not ready on day one. A supplier may need months to build millions of replacement parts, and dealers receive them in batches. During that time the manufacturer is supposed to tell owners what to do in the interim. If you are stuck waiting, our article on what to do when recall parts are not available covers the options.

7. Tracking completion

Manufacturers report recall completion rates to NHTSA every quarter for several quarters after a recall is filed. Many recalls never reach full completion: cars change hands, letters go to old addresses, and some owners simply never book the appointment. Every unrepaired car is one that still carries the original risk, which is the main reason this site exists — to make the notice easier to understand, so more people actually get the free repair done.

The short version

  1. A safety defect is found by the manufacturer, a supplier, or NHTSA, often from patterns in owner complaints.
  2. The manufacturer files a defect report within five business days and gets a campaign number.
  3. Owner letters go out within 60 days, with interim letters if the fix is not ready.
  4. Any franchised dealer for the brand performs the repair for free.
  5. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because letters can miss you.
